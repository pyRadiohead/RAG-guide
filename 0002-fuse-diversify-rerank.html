Lesson 02 · After Retrieval

Fuse, Diversify, Rerank

Chunking decides what can be found. These three stages decide what the LLM actually sees.

Why this matters for your mission: the best chunking numbers you saw in lesson 01 (Anthropic's 67% failure reduction) were only reached with rank fusion and reranking. You can't defend a chunking choice without knowing what the downstream pipeline will fix — and what it won't.

1The post-retrieval pipeline

After your chunks are embedded and indexed, a query kicks off this chain. Each colored stage is optional — but the order is not: cheap operations first, the expensive cross-encoder last, on the smallest list.

2Fuse — Reciprocal Rank Fusion

Hybrid search runs BM25 and vector search in parallel. Now you hold two ranked lists — and a trap: their scores are not comparable. BM25 scores are unbounded (12.4? 31.7?); cosine similarity lives in [−1, 1]. Summing or averaging raw scores is meaningless. This is the score normalization problem (OpenSearch).

RRF's move: throw the scores away, keep only the ranks.

RRF(d)  =  Σr ∈ retrievers   wr / ( k + rankr(d) ) rank starts at 1 · doc missing from a list contributes 0 for that list · canonical RRF has all wr = 1 (Cormack et al., SIGIR 2009)

Managing k — the only required knob

k dampens the advantage of rank #1 over rank #2. With k=0: rank 1 scores 1.0, rank 2 scores 0.5 — top hit dominates. With k=60: rank 1 scores 0.0164, rank 2 scores 0.0161 — nearly equal, deep ranks still count.

k valueEffectWhen
60 (default)Balanced; tuned on TREC data, generalizes wellStart here. Azure, Milvus, OpenSearch all default to it (Azure, Milvus)
low (1–10)Top ranks dominateShort final lists (top-5/10) where you trust each retriever's head
high (60–100)Flatter; consensus across lists winsLong candidate lists (top-100+), noisy retrievers

Managing weights — the optional knob

The weighted variant w_r / (k + rank) isn't canonical RRF, but it's everywhere in practice (BigDataBoutique). Strategy for managing it:

SituationWeights
Default — no eval data yet1 : 1. Don't tune blind.
Measured: vector clearly stronger on your eval setRaise vector, e.g. 0.7 : 1.3; sweep and re-measure
Exact-match domain (SKUs, codes, names, legal cites)Favor BM25
Paraphrase-heavy queries, conversationalFavor vector
! Never tune weights without an eval set. Build 50–200 queries with judged answers, measure Recall@k / NDCG@10 / MRR, then sweep k and weights. Equal weights + k=60 is usually within noise of the tuned optimum.

      4Diversify — Maximal Marginal Relevance

      Fusion and reranking share a blind spot: they score each document independently. If your corpus holds five near-copies of the same passage — and chunk overlap from lesson 01 manufactures exactly such near-copies — all five can fill your top-5. Relevant? Completely. Useful? You bought one fact five times.

      MMR (Carbonell & Goldstein, 1998) picks documents greedily, one at a time, rewarding relevance to the query and punishing similarity to what's already picked:

      pick   argmaxd [ λ·sim(d, query) − (1−λ)·maxs ∈ selected sim(d, s) ] λ=1 → pure relevance (MMR off) · λ=0 → pure diversity · λ≈0.7 is the practical default

      6Rerank — the cross-encoder

      Your retriever is a bi-encoder: it embeds query and document separately, then compares vectors. Fast — documents are embedded once, offline — but the model never reads query and document together, so nuance dies in compression.

      A cross-encoder reads the concatenated pair [query + document] through one transformer pass and outputs a single relevance score. Far more accurate — and far too slow to run against a whole corpus. Hence two-stage retrieval: bi-encoder casts a wide net (50–150 candidates), cross-encoder rescores just those, keep the top 5–20 (Pinecone).

      Bi-encoder (retriever)Cross-encoder (reranker)
      Reads query+doc together✗✓
      Corpus precomputable✓ (embed once)✗ (every query)
      Speedms over millions~100ms over ~100 docs
      Accuracybaseline>4 pts better avg on BEIR (Rosa et al., 2022)

      The payoff stacks with everything else: Anthropic measured contextual embeddings + contextual BM25 cutting retrieval failures by 49% — and adding reranking pushed that to 67% (Anthropic, 2024). Options: hosted APIs (Cohere Rerank, Voyage) or local open models (sentence-transformers cross-encoders, BGE-reranker) — the latter fits your no-framework constraint: it's one model.predict([(query, doc)]) call.

      ! Reranking can't resurrect what retrieval never returned. It reorders candidates; it doesn't add new ones. If recall is your problem (lesson 01), fix chunking/fusion first — rerank only converts good recall into good precision.

      And the order, with reasons: fuse first (free, widens the net) → rerank second (expensive, so run it on the fused shortlist, not the raw corpus) → MMR last (diversify the finalists, using the reranker's scores as the relevance term).

      8Check yourself

      1. A teammate merges hybrid results by summing the BM25 score (e.g. 14.2) with cosine similarity (e.g. 0.81) per doc. What's wrong?
      2. Your top-5 chunks are all near-identical copies of the same paragraph (thanks, 20% overlap). Reranking didn't help. Why not, and what does?
      3. Latency budget allows one cross-encoder pass over ~100 docs per query. Where in the pipeline do you spend it?
